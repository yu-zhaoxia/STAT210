2 Overall F-test

2.1 F-test for the Overall Significance of MLR

  • This test examines whether at least one predictor variable has a non-zero regression coefficient in the MLR model.
  • The null and alternative hypotheses are: \[\begin{align*} H_0: & \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \cdots = \beta_p = 0 \\ H_a: & \text{at least one } \beta_j \neq 0, j=1, 2, \ldots, p \end{align*}\]
  • The F-statistic compares the fit of the full model (with all predictor variables) to the reduced model (with only the intercept).

2.2 The Full Model

  • The full model is: \[y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{ip} + \epsilon_i\]

  • We denote the sum of squared errors (SSE) for the full model as \(SSE\): \[SSE=\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\]

2.3 The Reduced Model

  • The null hypothesis for the overall F-test in MLR is: \[H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \cdots = \beta_p = 0\]
  • The reduced model under the null hypothesis is: \[y_i = \beta_0 + \epsilon_i\]
  • The sum of squares errors for the reduced model is denoted as \(\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i -\bar y)^2\), which is the \(SST\).

2.4 The Difference in Fit between Full and Reduced Models

  • We will decompose the total variability in the response variable \(y\) into two components \[\begin{align*} SST & = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2 \\ & = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}_{SSE} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2}_{SSR} \end{align*}\]

  • Here,

    • \(SST\) is the total sum of squares, calculated from the reduced model;
    • \(SSE\) is the error sum of squares, calculated from full model;
    • \(SSR\) is the regression sum of squares. Note \(SSR=SST - SSE\).

2.5 The F-statistic for MLR

  • The F-statistic tests the following hypotheses: \[\begin{align*} H_0: & \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \cdots = \beta_p = 0 \\ H_a: & \text{at least one } \beta_j \neq 0, j=1, 2, \ldots, p \end{align*}\]
  • The F-statistic is given by: \[F = \frac{MSR}{MSE} = \frac{SSR/p}{SSE/(n-(p+1))}\] where
    • \(MSR\) is the mean square for regression,
    • \(MSE\) is the mean square for error,
    • \(p\) is the number of predictor variables,
    • \(n\) is the number of observations.

2.6 The Distribution of the F-statistic

  • Under \(H_0\), \(SSR/\sigma^2 \sim \chi_{p}^2\).
  • \(SSE/\sigma^2\sim \chi_{n-p-1}\).
  • \(SS\) and \(SSE\) are independent.
  • Therefore, \(F=\frac{MSR}{MSE} \overset{H_0}\sim F_{p, n-p-1}\).

3 Partial F-tests

3.1 Motivating Example: CDI

  • The dataset CDI gives various characteristics of 440 of the most populous counties in the USA.
  • We’ll try to predict y = unemployment percent from:
    • \(x_1\) = percent of those over 25 with a BS degree;
    • \(x_2\) = per capital income;
    • \(x_3\) = percent of population over 65.
  • The full model is \[y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \beta_3 x_{i3} + \epsilon_i\]

3.3 CDI Example

  • \(x_1\) (BSDegree) is significant predictor of unemployment.
  • \(x_2\) (Income) is not significant when \(x_1\) and \(x_3\) are in the model.
  • \(x_3\) (Percentage of population over 65) is not significant when \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are in the model.
  • We do not know whether both \(x_2\) and \(x_3\) should be excluded after including \(x_1\) in the model.

3.4 CDI Example

  • In this situation, it is not interesting to conduct the overall F-test because

  • We can still use F tests.

    • \(H_0: \beta_2=\beta_3=0\).
    • \(H_1:\) at least one of them is not zero.

  • The reduced model (corresponding to \(H_0\)) is \[y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \epsilon\]

  • The F-statistic is \[F=\frac{(1697.5- 1681.8)/(438 - 436)}{1681.8/436}=2.0365\]
  • The result suggest \(x_2\) and \(x_3\) are not needed in the model.

4 Extra Sums of Squares

4.1 Extra Sums of Squares

  • In multiple regression, we are often interested in how much one set of variables increases SSR (sum of squares for regression), given another set of variables already is in the model. The increment in SSR by adding a set of predictors (set 2) to an existing set of predictors (set 1) is \[SSR(\text{set 2} | \text{set 1}) = SSR(\text{set 1 and set 2}) - SSR(\text{set 1})\] or equivalently, \[SSR(\text{set 2} | \text{set 1}) = SSE(\text{set 1}) - SSE(\text{set 1 and set 2})\]

  • In the CDI example, the extra sum of squares due to adding \(x_2\) and \(x_3\) to a model that already contains \(x_1\) is \[SSR(x_2, x_3 | x_1) = SSE(x_1) - SSE(x_1, x_2, x_3) = 1697.5 - 1681.8 = 15.7\]

4.4 Partial F-test Using Extra Sums of Squares

  • The partial F-test for testing \(H_0: \beta_2 = \beta_3 = 0\) vs. \(H_a:\) at least one of them is not zero can be conducted using extra sums of squares: \[F = \frac{SSR(x_2, x_3 | x_1)/2}{MSE(\text{full model})} = \frac{15.7/2}{1681.8/436} = 2.0365\]
  • This is the same F-statistic as obtained before.

4.5 Decomposition of SSR

  • Recall from SLR, we have \[SST=SSR + SSE\] where \[SSR = \beta_1^2 \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2\]
  • In MLR with \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), \[SST=SSR(\beta_1, \beta_2) + SSE(\beta_1, \beta_2)\] and \[SST=SSR(\beta_1) + SSR(\beta_2|\beta_1) + SSE(\beta_1, \beta_2),\] or equivalently \[SST=SSR(\beta_2) + SSR(\beta_1|\beta_2) + SSE(\beta_1, \beta_2)\]

4.6 Decomposition of SSR

  • The order of predictors is aribitrary. For example, in a model with three predictors \(x_1, x_2, x_3\), we have \[\begin{align*} SST&=SSR(x_1) + SSR(x_2|x_1) + SSR(x_3|x_1, x_2) + SSE(x_1, x_2, x_3)\\ &=SSR(x_2) + SSR(x_1|x_2) + SSR(x_3|x_1, x_2) + SSE(x_1, x_2, x_3)\\ &=SSR(x_3) + SSR(x_1|x_3) + SSR(x_2|x_1, x_3) + SSE(x_1, x_2, x_3)\\ \cdots & \cdots \end{align*}\]

5.10 Conclusion

  • The partial F-test and the t-test give the same conclusion regarding the significance of individual predictors in the MLR model.

  • The predictor to be test should be the last one(s) entered in the model for the partial F-test.

  • Both tests are based on the same extra sums of squares and assesses the same null hypothesis - after adjusting for other predictors in the model, is the predictor still significant.
