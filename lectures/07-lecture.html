2 MLR

2.1 SLR vs MLR

  • SLR: In a simple linear regression (SLR) model, we have one response variable \(y\) and one predictor variable \(x\). \[y=\beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \epsilon\]

    • For individual \(i\), \(i=1, \ldots, n\), \[y_i= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \epsilon_i\]

  • MLR: In a multiple linear regression (MLR) model, we have one response variable \(t\) and multiple predictor variables \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_p\). \[y= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots, \beta_p x_p + \epsilon\]

2.2 MLR

  • For indivuidual \(i\), \(i=1, \ldots, n\), \[y_i= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{ip} + \epsilon_i\] where

  • \(y_i\) is the response variable for individual \(i\).

  • \(x_{ij}\) is the value of predictor variable \(j\) for individual \(i\).

  • \(\epsilon_i\)’s are errors:

    • The assumptions on \(\epsilon_i\)’s are the same as those in SLR.
    • weaker version: \(\epsilon_i\)’s are independent with mean 0 and constant variance \(\sigma^2\).
    • Strong version: \(\epsilon_i\)’s are i.i.d. \(N(0, \sigma^2)\).

  • The parameters to be estimated are \(\beta_0, \beta_1, \ldots, \beta_p\) and \(\sigma^2\).

2.3 Notations

  • When putting all individuals together, we have \[\begin{align*} y_1 &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{11} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{1p} + \epsilon_1 \\ y_2 &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{21} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{2p} + \epsilon_2 \\ &\vdots \\ y_n &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{n1} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{np} + \epsilon_n \end{align*}\]

2.4 Matrix Form

  • In matrix form, we have \[\begin{bmatrix}y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_n\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{1p} \\ 1 & x_{21} & x_{22} & \cdots & x_{2p} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_{n1} & x_{n2} & \cdots & x_{np}\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}\beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \\ \beta_2 \\ \vdots \\ \beta_p\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}\epsilon_1 \\ \epsilon_2 \\ \vdots \\ \epsilon_n\end{bmatrix}\]

2.5 Matrix Form

More compactly, \[\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X} \boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\]

here

  • \(\mathbf{y}\) is the response vector of size \(n \times 1\).

  • \(\mathbf{X}\) is the design matrix of size \(n \times (p+1)\).

  • \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is the parameter vector of size \((p+1) \times 1\).

  • \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) is the error vector of size \(n \times 1\), where \(E(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}) = \mathbf{0}\) and \(Var(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}) = \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}_n\). Here \(\sigma^2 \mathbf{I}_n\) is the variance covariance of \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) - it is a diagonal matrix with diagonal elements \(\sigma^2\).

2.6 Matrix Form

  • \(\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix}y_1 \\ \vdots \\ y_n\end{bmatrix}\) is the response vector of size \(n \times 1\).
  • \(\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{1p} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_{n1} & x_{n2} & \cdots & x_{np}\end{bmatrix}\) is the design matrix of size \(n \times (p+1)\).
  • \(\boldsymbol{\beta} = \begin{bmatrix}\beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \\ \vdots \\ \beta_p\end{bmatrix}\) is the parameter vector of size \((p+1) \times 1\).
  • \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon} = \begin{bmatrix}\epsilon_1 \\ \vdots \\ \epsilon_n\end{bmatrix}\) is the error vector of size \(n \times 1\).

3 Normal Equations

3.1 Least Squares Estimation (LSE) for MLR

  • The least squares estimation (LSE) of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is obtained by minimizing the residual sum of squares (RSS): \[RSS(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - E[y_i])^2 = (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})^T (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})\] where \(E[y_i] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{ip}\) is the fitted value for individual \(i\).

3.2 LSE for MLR: \(p=2\)

  • Let’s consider \(p=2\) for illustration: \[RSS(\beta_0, \beta_1, \beta_2) = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_{i1} - \beta_2 x_{i2})^2\]
  • To find the minimum, we take the partial derivatives with respect to \(\beta_0, \beta_1, \beta_2\) and set them to zero:

\[\begin{align*} \frac{\partial RSS}{\partial \beta_0} &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_{i1} - \beta_2 x_{i2}) = 0 \\ \frac{\partial RSS}{\partial \beta_1} &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i1} (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_{i1} - \beta_2 x_{i2}) = 0 \\ \frac{\partial RSS}{\partial \beta_2} &= -2 \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i2} (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_{i1} - \beta_2 x_{i2}) = 0 \end{align*}\]

3.3 Normal Equations: \(p=2\)

  • Note that there are three linear equations. They can be rewritten as: \[\begin{align*} n \beta_0 + \beta_1 \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i1} + \beta_2 \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i2} & = \sum_{i=1}^n y_i\\ \beta_0 \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i1} + \beta_1 \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i1}^2 + \beta_2 \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i1} x_{i2} & = \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i1} y_i\\ \beta_0 \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i2} + \beta_1 \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i1} x_{i2} + \beta_2 \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i2}^2 & = \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i2} y_i \end{align*}\]

  • These equations are called the normal equations.

3.4 Normal Equations: \(p=2\)

  • To connect the normal equations to their matrix form, we can write them as: \[\begin{bmatrix}n & \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i1} & \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i2} \\ \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i1} & \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i1}^2 & \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i1} x_{i2} \\ \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i2} & \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i1} x_{i2} & \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i2}^2\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}\beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \\ \beta_2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i \\ \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i1} y_i \\ \sum_{i=1}^n x_{i2} y_i\end{bmatrix}\]

3.5 Normal Equations: Matrix Form for \(p=2\)

Recall that

  • \(\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & x_{11} & x_{12}\\ 1 & x_{21} & x_{22}\\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots\\ 1 & x_{n1} & x_{n2}\end{bmatrix}\) is the design matrix of size \(n \times (p+1)\).

  • \(\boldsymbol{\beta}= \begin{bmatrix}\beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \\ \beta_2\end{bmatrix}\) is the parameter vector of size \((p+1) \times 1\).

  • \(\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix}y_1 \\ \vdots \\ y_n\end{bmatrix}\) is the response vector of size \(n \times 1\).

  • We can verify that the normal equations can be written as: \[\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} \boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y}\]

  • How to solve for \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\)?

4 LSE and Fitted Values

4.1 Normal Equations: Matrix Form for general \(p\)

  • For general \(p\), the normal equations can be written as: \[\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} \boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y}\]

  • If \(\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X}\) is invertible, the LSE of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is given by: \[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} =\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\beta}_0 \\ \hat{\beta}_1 \\ \vdots \\ \hat{\beta}_p\end{bmatrix} =(\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y}\]

4.2 Matrix Form Deriviation of LSE

4.3 MLR: fitted values

  • The fitted values can be computed as: \[\hat{\mathbf{y}} =\begin{bmatrix}\hat{y}_1 \\ \hat{y}_2 \\ \vdots \\ \hat{y}_n\end{bmatrix} =\mathbf{X} \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{X} (\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y}\]

  • The hat matrix: \[\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{X} (\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^T\]

Note that \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}= \mathbf{H} \mathbf{y}\).

5 Residuals

  • The residuals can be computed as: \[\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} = \begin{bmatrix}\hat{\epsilon}_1 \\ \hat{\epsilon}_2 \\ \vdots \\ \hat{\epsilon}_n\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}y_1 - \hat{y}_1 \\ y_2 - \hat{y}_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_n - \hat{y}_n\end{bmatrix} =\mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}= (\mathbf{I}_n - \mathbf{H}) \mathbf{y}\]

5.1 SSE and MSE

  • The sum of squared errors (SSE) can be computed as: \[\begin{align*} SSE & = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i -\hat y_i)^2 = \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^T \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} = (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^T (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\\ &=[(\mathbf{I}_n - \mathbf{H}) \mathbf{y}]^T (\mathbf{I}_n - \mathbf{H}) \mathbf{y}\\ &=\mathbf{y}^T (\mathbf{I}_n - \mathbf{H}) \mathbf{y} \\ \end{align*}\]

  • The mean squared error (MSE) is given by: \[MSE = \frac{SSE}{n-(p+1)}\] where \(n-(p+1)\) is the degrees of freedom (d.f.) for error.

  • An unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\) is given by \(\hat{\sigma}^2 = MSE\).

6.1 Example: Heights

  • This is a simulated data set to practice MLR.
Code 
set.seed(42); n <- 50
library(MASS)
parents <- mvrnorm(n,
  mu    = c(69.1, 63.7),                 
  Sigma = matrix(c(3^2, 0.2*3*2.8,
                   0.2*3*2.8, 2.8^2), 2, 2))
f.height <- parents[,1]; m.height <- parents[,2]

sex     <- sample(c("son","daughter"), n, TRUE)
sex_eff <- ifelse(sex=="son", 2.7, -2.7)
midp    <- (f.height + m.height)/2
c.height <- (69.1 - 0.60*((69.1+63.7)/2) - 2.7) + 
  0.60*midp + sex_eff + rnorm(n, 0, 2.5)
height.data= data.frame(c.height, f.height, m.height)

7 Inference

7.1 Statistical Inference About Regression Parameters

  • Under the strong version of the model assumptions, we have: \[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \sim N(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \sigma^2 (\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X})^{-1})\]
  • The variance (var-cov) of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) can be estimated by: \[\widehat{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = s^2 (\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X})^{-1}\]
  • The standard error (SE) of \(\hat{\beta}_j\) is given by: \[SE(\hat{\beta}_j) = s \sqrt{ \cdot [(\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X})^{-1}]_{jj}}\] where \([(\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X})^{-1}]_{jj}\) is the \(j\)-th diagonal element of the matrix \((\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X})^{-1}\).

7.2 t-tests For Regression Parameters

  • To test the significance of each predictor variable, we can use the t-test: \[t = \frac{\hat{\beta}_j - \beta_{j0}}{SE(\hat{\beta}_j)}\] where \(\beta_{j0}\) is the hypothesized value of \(\beta_j\) under the null hypothesis.
  • Under the null hypothesis \(H_0: \beta_j = \beta_{j0}\), the test statistic follows a t-distribution with \(n-(p+1)\) degrees of freedom: \[t \sim t_{n-(p+1)}\]

7.3 t-tests For Regression Parameters

  • We mentioned that \(\hat\beta_j\) follows a normal distribution. To construct a t statistic, we also need the following.
  • It can be shown that \(\hat\beta_j\) and \(s^2\) are independent, and \[\frac{(n-(p+1))s^2}{\sigma^2} = \frac{SSE}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-(p+1)}\]
  • Therefore, we have \[\frac{\hat{\beta}_j - \beta_j}{SE(\hat{\beta}_j)} = \frac{\hat{\beta}_j - \beta_j}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 [(\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X})^{-1}]_{jj}}} \bigg/ \sqrt{\frac{(n-(p+1))s^2}{\sigma^2 (n-(p+1))}} \sim t_{n-(p+1)}\]

7.4 Confidence Intervals For Regression Parameters

  • A \((1-\alpha)100\%\) confidence interval for \(\beta_j\) is given by: \[\hat{\beta}_j \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-(p+1)} \cdot SE(\hat{\beta}_j)\] where \(t_{\alpha/2, n-(p+1)}\) is the \((1-\alpha/2)\) quantile of the t-distribution with \(n-(p+1)\) degrees of freedom.

  • This confidence is derived from the t-test statistic, as follows: \[\begin{align*} 1-\alpha=P\left(-t_{\alpha/2, n-(p+1)} \leq \frac{\hat{\beta}_j - \beta_j}{SE(\hat{\beta}_j)} \leq t_{\alpha/2, n-(p+1)}\right) &\\ = P\left(\hat{\beta}_j - t_{\alpha/2, n-(p+1)} SE(\hat{\beta}_j) \leq \beta_j \leq \hat{\beta}_j + t_{\alpha/2, n-(p+1)} SE(\hat{\beta}_j)\right)& \end{align*}\]
