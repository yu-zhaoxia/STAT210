Lec 02: Inference In Simple Linear Regressionn

1 Intro

1.0.1 Review: Lecture 01

  • In lecture 01, we introduced the simple linear regression model and the least squares estimation (LSE) method.

  • LSE: \[\text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 x_i)^2\] \[\{\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1\} = \arg\min_{\beta_0, \beta_1} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i -\beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i)^2\]

  • The LSEs are \[\hat\beta_1 =\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}, \quad \hat\beta_0 = \bar{y} - \hat\beta_1 \bar{x}.\]

1.0.2 Review: Lecture 02

  • In lecture 02, we discussed distribution, confidence interval, and hypothesis testing related to \(\beta_1\). In particular, we showed that
  • \[\hat\beta_1 =\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\]
  • \(Var(\hat\beta_1)= \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{XX}}\), where \(S_{XX}=\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2\)
  • \[T=\frac{\hat\beta_1 - \beta_1}{s/\sqrt{S_{xx}}} =\frac{\hat\beta_1 - \beta_1}{se(\hat\beta_1)} \sim t_{n-2}\]

where \(se(\hat{\beta_1}) =\sqrt{\hat{Var}(\hat\beta_1)}= \sqrt{\frac{s^2}{S_{XX}}}\) and \(s^2 = \frac{SSE}{n-2}\).

1.0.3 Outline

  • In this lecture, we will continue to discuss inference. We will focus on
    • estimating a mean response
    • predicting a new response
    • analysis of variance (ANOVA)

2 Distribution of \(\hat\beta_0\)

2.0.1 Distribution of \(\hat\beta_0\)

  • Recall the simple linear regression model: \[y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \epsilon_i, \quad \epsilon_i \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(0, \sigma^2), \quad i=1,2,\ldots,n.\]

  • The least squares estimator of \(\beta_0\) is \[\hat\beta_0 = \bar{y} - \hat\beta_1 \bar{x}.\]

  • The expectation of \(\hat\beta_0\) is \[\begin{align*} E(\hat\beta_0) &= E(\bar{y} - \hat\beta_1 \bar{x}) \\ &= E(\bar{y}) - \bar{x} E(\hat\beta_1) \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 \bar{x} - \bar{x} \beta_1 = \beta_0. \end{align*}\]

2.0.2 Distribution of \(\hat\beta_0\)

  • \(\hat\beta_0\) is a linear function of \(y_i\)’s: \[\begin{align*} \hat\beta_0 &= \bar{y} - \hat\beta_1 \bar{x} \\ &= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n y_i - \frac{\bar{x}}{S_{XX}} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x}) y_i \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \frac{1}{n} - \frac{\bar{x}}{S_{XX}} (x_i - \bar{x}) \right) y_i. \end{align*}\]

  • Since \(\hat\beta_0\) is a linear combination of \(y_i\)’s, it is normally distributed.

  • We have already find its mean (the LSE of \(\beta_0\) is unbiased). Next, we will find its variance.

2.0.3 Variance of \(\hat\beta_0\)

  • The variance of \(\hat\beta_0\) is \[\begin{align*} \text{Var}(\hat\beta_0) &= \text{Var}\left( \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \frac{1}{n} - \frac{\bar{x}}{S_{XX}} (x_i - \bar{x}) \right) y_i \right) \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \frac{1}{n} - \frac{\bar{x}}{S_{XX}} (x_i - \bar{x}) \right)^2 \text{Var}(y_i) \\ &= \sigma^2 \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \frac{1}{n} - \frac{\bar{x}}{S_{XX}} (x_i - \bar{x}) \right)^2. \end{align*}\]
  • After some algebra, we have \[\text{Var}(\hat\beta_0) = \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{XX}} \right).\]

2.0.4 Distribution of \(\hat\beta_0\)

  • We have shown that \(\hat\beta_0\) is a linear combination of \(y_i\)’s, so it is normally distributed. We have also found its mean and variance. Therefore, \[\hat\beta_0 \sim N\left( \beta_0, \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{XX}} \right) \right).\]
  • When inference of \(\hat\beta_0\) is needed, we can construct a t-statistic.
  • The t-statistic for \(\beta_0\) is \[t = \frac{\hat\beta_0 - \beta_0}{s \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{XX}}}} \sim t_{n-2},\] where \(s^2 = \text{SSE}/(n-2)\) is the unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\).
  • Confidence interval and hypothesis testing for \(\beta_0\) can be conducted based on the above t-statistic.

2.0.5 Variance of \(\hat\beta_0\): Method 2

  • We present a different way to find \(\text{Var}(\hat\beta_0)\). \[\begin{align*} \text{Var}(\hat\beta_0) &= \text{Var}(\bar{y} - \hat\beta_1 \bar{x}) \\ &= \text{Var}(\bar{y}) + \bar{x}^2 \text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) - 2\bar{x} \text{Cov}(\bar{y}, \hat\beta_1). \end{align*}\]

  • Need to find \(\text{Var}(\bar{y})\) and \(\text{Cov}(\bar{y}, \hat\beta_1)\).

  • Variance of \(\bar{y}\):

\(\text{Var}(\bar{y})=\text{Var}(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i ) = \frac{1}{n^2} n \sigma^2 = \sigma^2/n\)

2.0.6 Covariance of \(\bar{y}\) and \(\hat\beta_1\)

2.0.7 Variance of \(\hat\beta_0\): Method 2

Putting everything together, we have \[\begin{align*} \text{Var}(\hat\beta_0) &= \text{Var}(\bar{y}) + \bar{x}^2 \text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) - 2\bar{x} \text{Cov}(\bar{y}, \hat\beta_1) \\ &= \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \bar{x}^2 \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{XX}} - 2\bar{x} \cdot 0 \\ &= \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{XX}} \right). \end{align*}\] This is the same result as before.

3 Cov(\(\hat\beta_0\), \(\hat\beta_1\))

3.0.1 Covariance of \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\)

  • In many inference problems, we need the covariance of \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\). With normality assumptions, we can find the joint distribution of \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\).

  • Covariance of \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\): \[\begin{align*} \text{Cov}(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1) &= \text{Cov}(\bar y - \hat\beta_1 \bar x,\; \hat\beta_1) \\ &= \text{Cov}(\bar y,\hat\beta_1) - \bar x\,\text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) \\ &= 0 - \bar x \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{XX}} \quad\text{(from previous results)}\\ &= -\frac{\bar x \sigma^2}{S_{XX}}. \end{align*}\]

4 Mean Response

4.0.1 Mean Response

  • A common interest in regression analysis is to estimate the mean response at a given value of \(x\), say \(x_h\).
  • The mean response at \(x_h\) is \[\mu_{y|x_h} = E(y|x=x_h) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_h.\]
  • The point estimator of \(\mu_{y|x_h}\) is \[\hat\mu_{y|x_h} = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_h.\]

4.0.2 Distribution of \(\hat\mu_{y|x_h}\)

  • Since \(\hat\mu_{y|x_h}\) is a linear combination of \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\), therefore a linear combination of \(y_i\)’s (which are independently normal). Thus, \(\hat\mu_{y|x_h}\) is normally distributed.
  • The expectation of \(\hat\mu_{y|x_h}\) is \[\begin{align*} E(\hat\mu_{y|x_h}) &= E(\hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_h) \\ &= E(\hat\beta_0) + x_h E(\hat\beta_1) \\ &= \beta_0 + x_h \beta_1 = \mu_{y|x_h}. \end{align*}\]

4.0.3 Distribution of \(\hat\mu_{y|x_h}\)

  • The variance of \(\hat\mu_{y|x_h}\) is \[\begin{align*} \text{Var}(\hat\mu_{y|x_h}) &= \text{Var}(\hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_h) \\ &= \text{Var}(\hat\beta_0) + x_h^2 \text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) + 2 x_h \text{Cov}(\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1). \end{align*}\]
  • Plugging in the previous results, we have \[\begin{align*} \text{Var}(\hat\mu_{y|x_h}) &= \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{XX}} \right) + x_h^2 \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{XX}} + 2 x_h \left( -\frac{\bar{x} \sigma^2}{S_{XX}} \right) \\ &= \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_h - \bar{x})^2}{S_{XX}} \right). \end{align*}\]
  • Therefore, \[\hat\mu_{y|x_h} \sim N\left( \mu_{y|x_h}, \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_h - \bar{x})^2}{S_{XX}} \right) \right).\]

4.0.4 Confidence Interval for Mean Response

  • Since \(\hat\mu_{y|x_h}\) is normally distributed, we can construct a t-statistic for \(\mu_{y|x_h}\):

\[t = \frac{\hat\mu_{y|x_h} - \mu_{y|x_h}}{s \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_h - \bar{x})^2}{S_{XX}}}} \sim t_{n-2},\] where \(s^2 = \text{SSE}/(n-2)\) is the unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\).

  • A \((1-\alpha)100\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu_{y|x_h}\) is \[\hat\mu_{y|x_h} \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-2} \cdot s \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_h - \bar{x})^2}{S_{XX}}},\] where \(t_{\alpha/2, n-2}\) is the \((1-\alpha/2)\) quantile of the \(t\) distribution with \(n-2\) degrees of freedom.

5 Prediction of a New Response

5.0.1 Prediction of a New Response

  • Another common interest in regression analysis is to predict a new response at a given value of \(x\), say \(x_h\).
  • The new response at \(x_h\) is \[y_{\text{new}} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_h + \textcolor{red}{\epsilon},\] where \(\epsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\) is independent of the \(\epsilon_i\)’s in the original model.
  • The point predictor of \(y_{\text{new}}\) is \[\hat{y}_{\text{new}} = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_h.\]

5.0.2 Mean and Variance of \(\hat{y}_{\text{new}}\)

  • Note that \(\hat{y}_{\text{new}}\) is the same as \(\hat\mu_{y|x_h}\), the point estimator of the mean response at \(x_h\).

  • They have the same mean and variance.

  • To construct a prediction interval for \(y_{\text{new}}\), we need to account for the extra variability due to \(\epsilon\).

  • Technically, we need to find the distribution of \[\hat{y}_{\text{new}} - y_{\text{new}}\]

5.0.3 Distribution of \(\hat{y}_{\text{new}} - y_{\text{new}}\)

\[\begin{align*} \hat{y}_{\text{new}} - y_{\text{new}} &= (\hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_h) - (\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_h + \epsilon) \\ &= (\hat\beta_0 - \beta_0) + (\hat\beta_1 - \beta_1) x_h - \epsilon. \end{align*}\]

  • Mean: \[\begin{align*} E[\hat{y}_{\text{new}} - y_{\text{new}}] &= E(\hat\beta_0 - \beta_0) + x_h E(\hat\beta_1 - \beta_1) - E(\epsilon) \\ &= 0 + x_h \cdot 0 - 0 = 0. \end{align*}\]

5.0.4 Distribution of \(\hat{y}_{\text{new}} - y_{\text{new}}\)

  • Variance: \[\begin{align*} Var(\hat{y}_{\text{new}} - y_{\text{new}}) &= Var(\hat{y}_{\text{new}}) + Var(y_{\text{new}}) + 0\\ &= \sigma^2 \left(\frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_h - \bar{x})^2}{S_{XX}} \right) + \textcolor{red}{\sigma^2}\\ &= \sigma^2 \left(\textcolor{red}{ 1} + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_h - \bar{x})^2}{S_{XX}} \right). \end{align*}\]

The first step is true because \(y_{\text{new}}\) is a new data point, thus is independent of \(\hat\mu_{y|x_h}\), which replies on the old data.

5.0.5 Distribution of \(\hat{y}_{\text{new}} - y_{\text{new}}\)

  • Note that the variance of \(\hat{y}_{\text{new}} - y_{\text{new}}\) has an extra \(\sigma^2\) term compared to the variance of \(\hat\mu_{y|x_h}\).

  • This extra \(\sigma^2\) term accounts for the variability of the new observation \(y_{\text{new}}\) due to the random error \(\epsilon\).

  • We already explained that \(\hat\mu_{y|x_h}\) follows a normal distribution.

  • \(y_{\text{new}}\) also follows a normal distribution and it is independent of \(\hat\mu_{y|x_h}\) because it is a new data point.

5.0.6 Distribution of \(\hat{y}_{\text{new}} - y_{\text{new}}\)

  • As a result, \(\hat{y}_{\text{new}} - y_{\text{new}}\) also follows a normal distribution.

  • The standardized version is \[\frac{\hat{y}_{\text{new}} - y_{\text{new}}}{\sigma\sqrt{\textcolor{red}{1} + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_h - \bar x)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\bar x)^2}}}\] which follows a standard normal distribution.

  • But we don’t know \(\sigma\), so we need to estimate it by \(s=\sqrt{\text{SSE}/(n-2)}\).

  • The resulting distribution is a t-distribution with \(n-2\) degrees of freedom.

5.0.7 Prediction Interval

  • We explained that \[t = \frac{\hat{Y}_{\text{new}} - Y_{\text{new}}}{s \sqrt{\textcolor{red}{1} + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_h - \bar{X})^2}{S_{XX}}}} \sim t_{n-2},\] where \(s^2 = \text{SSE}/(n-2)\) is the unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\).

  • A \((1-\alpha)100\%\) prediction interval for \(Y_{\text{new}}\) is \[\hat{Y}_{\text{new}} \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-2} \cdot s \sqrt{\textcolor{red}{1} + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_h - \bar{X})^2}{S_{XX}}},\] where \(t_{\alpha/2, n-2}\) is the \((1-\alpha/2)\) quantile of the \(t\) distribution with \(n-2\) degrees of freedom.

7 ANOVA in SLR

7.0.1 Sum of Squares Decomposition

  • Total Sum of Squares (SST): \[SST = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2\]

    The total variation in the response variable.

  • Regression Sum of Squares (SSR): \[SSR = \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2\] The variation explained by the predictor (regression model).

  • Error Sum of Squares (SSE): \[SSE = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\] The variation not explained by the model (regression).

7.0.3 Decomposition of the Total Sum of Squares

It can be shown that: \[SST = SSR + SSE\]

Proof: \[\begin{align*} SST &= \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2 \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n \big[(y_i - \hat{y}_i) + (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})\big]^2 \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 + \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2 + 2\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)(\hat{y}_i - \bar{y}) \\ &= SSE + SSR + 2\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)(\hat{y}_i - \bar{y}) \end{align*}\]

7.0.5 ANOVA Table for SLR

  • DF: degrees of freedom
  • SS: sum of squares
  • MS: mean square (SS divided by corresponding DF)
  • F: F-statistic for testing \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) vs. \(H_a: \beta_1 \neq 0\)
  • Under \(H_0\), \(F \sim F_{1, n-2}\).

7.0.6 Expected Mean Squares

  • We will rewrite SSR in terms of \(\hat\beta_1\):

\[\begin{align*} SSR &= \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2 \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 X_i - \bar{y})^2 \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat\beta_1 (X_i - \bar{X}))^2 \quad\text{(since $\hat\beta_0 = \bar{y} - \hat\beta_1 \bar{X}$)}\\ &= \hat\beta_1^2 \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 = \hat\beta_1^2 S_{XX}. \end{align*}\] - Therefore, \[MSR = SSR/1 = \hat\beta_1^2 S_{XX}.\]

7.0.7 Expected Mean Squares

  • We find that \(SSR=\hat\beta_1^2 S_{XX}\). But \(\hat\beta_1 \sim N(\beta_1, \sigma^2/S_{XX})\). Note that \(Var(\hat\beta_1) = E(\hat\beta_1^2) - E^2[\hat\beta_1]\). Therefore, \[\begin{align*} E[SSR] &= E[\hat\beta_1^2 S_{XX}] \\ &= S_{XX} E[\hat\beta_1^2] \\ &= S_{XX} \left( \text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) + (E[\hat\beta_1])^2 \right) \\ &= S_{XX} \left( \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{XX}} + \beta_1^2 \right) \\ &= \sigma^2 + \beta_1^2 S_{XX}. \end{align*}\]

And \(E[MSR] = E[SSR/1] = \sigma^2 + \beta_1^2 S_{XX}\).

7.0.8 Expected Mean Squares

  • It can be shown that \[E(MSE) = \sigma^2.\]
  • When \(\beta_1=0\), \(E(MSR) = E(MSE) = \sigma^2\).
  • When \(\beta_1 \neq 0\), \(E(MSR) > E(MSE)\).
  • This justifies the F-test for testing \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) vs. \(H_a: \beta_1 \neq 0\).

7.0.9 F Test

  • For testing \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) vs. \(H_a: \beta_1 \neq 0\), the F-statistic is \[F = \frac{MSR}{MSE}.\]
  • We reject \(H_0\) if \(F\) is too large
    • The rejection region is \(F > F_{\alpha, 1, n-2}\), where \(F_{\alpha, 1, n-2}\) is the \((1-\alpha)\) quantile of the \(F\) distribution with 1 and \(n-2\) degrees of freedom.
  • The p-value is \(P(F_{1, n-2} \geq F_{\text{obs}})\), where \(F_{\text{obs}}\) is the observed value of the F-statistic.

7.0.10 F test and t-test

  • The F-statistic can be rewritten as \[F = \frac{MSR}{MSE} = \frac{\hat\beta_1^2 S_{XX}}{s^2} = \left( \frac{\hat\beta_1}{s/\sqrt{S_{XX}}} \right)^2 = t^2,\] where \(t = \hat\beta_1 / (s/\sqrt{S_{XX}})\) is the t-statistic for testing \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\).

  • Thus, the F-test and the t-test for testing \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) are equivalent in SLR.

  • In fact, as \(F_{1,n-2}\) distribution is also equivalent to the distribution of the square of a random variable whose distribution is \(t_{n-2}\), the F test is equivalent to the two-sided t test.
